A Greening Earth

A virtual tour of a more hopeful outlook on the greenery of our planet.

Human activity has placed pressure on ecosystems around the world, and many people may expect the planet to be steadily losing vegetation.

Surprisingly, MODIS satellite data shows that large areas became greener between 2000 and 2025. But this progress is uneven, and some regions still face deforestation, drought, and land degradation.

About this project

Mapping a Greener World

This interactive visualization uses MODIS Normalized Difference Vegetation Index data to compare vegetation patterns in 2000, 2013, and 2025. Many people may expect environmental pressure to have caused vegetation loss almost everywhere. However, the satellite record reveals a more hopeful and complex story: large parts of the world have become greener.

This progress is not identical in every region. Some areas show vegetation recovery, rainfall-driven growth, improved farming, or the effects of large restoration programs, while other areas continue to experience deforestation, drought, and land degradation. Therefore, this project invites readers to explore both the encouraging global trend and the regional challenges that remain.

Why this matters

The green and orange spikes represent more than satellite pixels. They reflect changing forests, farms, grasslands, habitats, water resources, and communities. The visualization shows that environmental improvement is possible and that restoration and protection efforts can make a meaningful difference.

How to interpret the maps

On the vegetation maps, higher NDVI generally indicates greener, denser, or healthier plant cover. On the change map, green cells represent an increase in NDVI and orange cells represent a decrease. Spike height represents vegetation intensity on the vegetation maps and the magnitude of change on the change map.

Data

The project uses satellite-based NDVI data derived from NASA MODIS vegetation products. The visualization is intended for exploration and broad comparison rather than precise measurement of forest cover or land-use change.

Step 1 of 5

A planet under pressure

Human activity has transformed forests, grasslands, farms, and drylands around the world. Many people may expect these pressures to have caused vegetation to decline almost everywhere.

Final takeaway

The Earth can become greener, but it's up to us.

From 2000 to 2025, many parts of the world show increasing vegetation. This gives us reason for optimism: natural recovery, rainfall, improved land management, and restoration programs can all contribute to a greener landscape.

And

Vegetation is increasing in many places

The global change map reveals widespread areas of vegetation growth. Regional examples such as the Sahel and Northern China show that recovery and restoration can produce visible results.

But

Greening has different meanings

The Amazon, Sahel, and Northern China each show a different combination of natural recovery, human action, and continuing environmental pressure. One global average cannot explain every region.

Therefore

Local understanding makes progress possible

The most important lesson is not simply that the Earth is greener. Environmental improvement is possible when restoration and protection respond to the conditions of each place.

Why this visualization reveals the takeaway

The globe first shows the surprising global pattern. The comparison view lets readers examine vegetation across years, while the change view separates growth from decline. Regional stories and charts then explain how the same global trend can have different causes and consequences in different places.

By moving from the global picture to regional evidence, the visualization allows readers to discover the takeaway rather than simply being told what to believe.
